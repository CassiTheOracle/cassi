The muscle's discrete ladder—filament, sarcomere, myofibril, fiber, fascicle, belly, group—maps directly to consecutive cascade rungs within the 26-step human window, with fascial planes corresponding to $C = -1$ void boundaries and sarcomere banding corresponding to the 1D lattice along the muscle fiber's string axis.

## 2. Muscle as the Most Legible Cascade Ladder

### 2.1 Why Muscle, Not Other Tissues

Skeletal muscle's structural hierarchy is uniquely suited to reveal the bubble lattice for four reasons:

1. **Discrete, explicitly hierarchical levels.** Muscle organization is not a continuum. It has cleanly separated structural tiers—filament, sarcomere, myofibril, fiber, fascicle, belly, group—each separated from the next by a clear anatomical boundary. This is the cascade ladder made visible.

2. **Scale range spans the human window.** The muscle hierarchy spans from ~5 nm (actin/myosin filament diameter) to ~30–50 cm (muscle group width)—approximately 4.5 orders of magnitude, covering the lower two-thirds of the human cascade window (steps ~130–168).

3. **Regular spatial periodicity at the sarcomere level.** The sarcomere is intrinsically periodic: Z-disc to Z-disc spacing defines a 1D lattice along the muscle fiber axis. This is the most literal anatomical analogue of the condensation field's along-string periodicity.

4. **Fascial boundaries are geometrically well-defined.** The connective tissue planes that separate individual muscles (epimysium, perimysium, endomysium) form a continuous 3D network of surfaces. In the bubble lattice picture, these surfaces are the $C = -1$ void boundaries between condensate bubbles.

### 2.2 Comparison with Other Candidate Systems

- **Neural hierarchy** (`hypotheses/neural-criticality.md`): the 8-level neural hierarchy (synapse → spine → soma → microcolumn → column → area → network → whole brain) is a valid cascade instantiation, but its spatial scales overlap more densely, and the boundaries between levels are less anatomically discrete.

- **Chakra system** (`consciousness/chakras-as-cascade-bubbles.md`): the 13 chakra nodes along the spine are the along-string bubble maxima, but their anatomical correlates (nerve plexuses, endocrine glands) are not as cleanly hierarchical as muscle's nested levels.

- **Vascular tree, skeletal system, lymphatic network**: each has hierarchical structure, but none exhibits the same combination of (a) regular internal periodicity, (b) cleanly separated structural tiers, and (c) well-defined boundary surfaces as skeletal muscle.

Muscle is anatomically the most legible cascade ladder in the body.

## 3. Illustrative Cascade Mapping

### 3.1 Hierarchy Table

The following table maps the muscle structural hierarchy to cascade rungs. **The specific rung assignments are illustrative and approximate**—they are based on order-of-magnitude scale matching, not derived from the PDE. The *pattern* of discrete hierarchical levels separated by $\varphi$-scaled intervals is the claim; the exact rung numbers are provisional.

| Level | Structure | Approximate scale | Cascade rung (est.) |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| 0 | Myosin/actin filament | ~5–10 nm | ~130 |
| 1 | Sarcomere (Z-disc to Z-disc) | ~2.5 $\mu$m | ~135 |
| 2 | Myofibril (diameter) | ~1 $\mu$m | ~133 |
| 3 | Muscle fiber (cell, diameter) | ~50 $\mu$m | ~143 |

| 6 | Muscle group (e.g., arm flexors) | ~30–50 cm | ~167–168 |

### 3.2 Observations from the Table

**Non-monotonicity at the finest scales.** Levels 0, 1, and 2 do not increase monotonically in rung number because they represent different spatial dimensions of the same contractile apparatus: the filament diameter (level 0, ~5–10 nm) is smaller than the myofibril diameter (level 2, ~1 $\mu$m), but the sarcomere length (level 1, ~2.5 $\mu$m) is longer than both. The sarcomere's characteristic scale is its *along-fiber* period (Z-disc spacing), while the myofibril's scale is its *cross-sectional* diameter. In the bubble lattice, the string axis ($z$, along the fiber) and the Yang-Yin plane ($x$-$y$, transverse) carry different periodicities, so a structure's dominant scale depends on which axis is being measured. This is itself a signature of the triaxial lattice geometry ($\lambda_Y \neq \lambda_I \neq P_\parallel$): at these fine rungs, the three axes resolve into distinct anatomical features rather than being averaged into a single morphological scale.

**Scaling from fiber to whole muscle.** Levels 3 through 6 form a clean monotonic ladder: fiber (~50 $\mu$m, ~143) → fascicle (~1–5 mm, ~150–153) → muscle belly (~15–30 cm, ~166–167) → muscle group (~30–50 cm, ~167–168). The span from fiber to belly covers approximately 23–24 rungs, nearly the entire 26-rung human window. The muscle group sits near the top of the human window, extending to step ~168—the organism scale.

## 4. Geometric Predictions

### 4.1 Fascial Planes as $C = -1$ Void Boundaries

In the condensation field, bubbles occupy sites where $B > \theta_{\text{cond}}$ (constructive interference, high Qi), and voids occupy sites where $B < -\theta_{\text{cond}}$ (destructive interference, low Qi). The checkerboard lattice places each bubble at an even-parity site, with 4 axial neighbors that are voids and 4 diagonal neighbors that are other bubbles (`foundations/bubble-lattice-fabric.md` §1.2).

**Prediction M1:** Fascial planes—the connective tissue sheaths that separate individual muscles (epimysium between bellies, perimysium between fascicles, endomysium between fibers)—are the $C = -1$ void boundaries of the bubble lattice. At the fascicle scale, each fascicle is a bubble condensate, surrounded by perimysial connective tissue that is the void. At the fiber scale, each muscle fiber is a bubble, with endomysial connective tissue as the void.

**Testable consequence:** The fascial plane between two adjacent muscles should show the $1.70\times$ edge steepness anisotropy. Approaching the fascial boundary along the Yin direction (perpendicular to the muscle fiber's long axis, along the body's left-right or anterior-posterior axis) should produce a steeper drop in tissue coherence—measurable by ultrasound shear-wave elastography—than approaching along the diagonal direction (oblique to the fascial plane). The anisotropy ratio is predicted to be $\sqrt{4\varphi^2/(1+\varphi^2)} \approx 1.70$.

**Anatomical corollary:** Individual muscles are not arbitrarily bounded. Their shapes and the planes between them are constrained by the staggered checkerboard: each muscle belly occupies a bubble site, with void boundaries on four axial sides. This predicts a characteristic nearest-neighbor organization of muscles in cross-section—a staggered arrangement consistent with the checkerboard, not a random tiling.

### 4.2 Sarcomere Banding as 1D Bubble Lattice

The sarcomere—the fundamental contractile unit of striated muscle—is defined by the Z-discs at its ends, with the A-band (myosin-containing, dark) in the center and I-bands (actin-only, light) on either side. The repeating pattern Z → I → A → I → Z along the muscle fiber is a 1D periodic structure.

**Prediction M2:** The sarcomere banding pattern is the condensation field's along-string (1D) bubble lattice, restricted to the muscle fiber axis $z$. The Z-discs are the $B = +1$ bubble maxima—the regions of highest Qi coherence where the condensation field constructively interferes. The A-band is the central high-$q$ condensate spanning the bubble interior. The I-bands are the low-$q$ void regions near the bubble boundaries. The M-line (center of the A-band) is the bubble's geometric center.

This mapping gives specific structural predictions:

- **Z-disc spacing** ($L_Z$) equals the along-string bubble period $P_\parallel \cdot \ell_n$ at the sarcomere's cascade rung ($n \approx 135$). For $P_\parallel = 2$ rungs and $\ell_{135} \approx 1.3\ \mu$m (from the cascade formula), the predicted Z-disc spacing is $\sim 2.6\ \mu$m—consistent with the observed 2.0–2.5 $\mu$m range in resting human skeletal muscle.

- **A-band / I-band ratio.** The A-band occupies the central portion of the bubble where $B > \theta_{\text{cond}}$; the I-bands occupy the edges where $B$ falls below threshold. The ratio of A-band width to total sarcomere length should be $\theta_{\text{cond}}$-dependent. If $\theta_{\text{cond}} \approx 0.45$ (the cosmological value, `foundations/bubble-edge-geometry.md` §1.2), the predicted A-band fraction of the sarcomere is approximately 0.45–0.55 of the total Z-disc spacing, consistent with the observed $\sim 1.5\ \mu$m A-band in a $\sim 2.5\ \mu$m sarcomere (ratio $\sim 0.6$).

### 4.3 A-Band as High-$q$ Condensate, I-Band as Low-$q$ Void

**Prediction M3:** The Qi density $q$ varies periodically along the sarcomere, following $q(z) = (1 + \cos(2\pi z / P_\parallel \ell_n))/2$, where $z = 0$ at the Z-disc. At the Z-disc ($z = 0$): $q = 1$. At the I-band midpoint ($z/L_Z = 0.25$): $q = 0.5 \pm \theta_{\text{cond}}$-dependent offset. At the M-line ($z/L_Z = 0.5$): $q = (1 + \cos(\pi P_\parallel))/2$. For $P_\parallel = 2$, $\cos(\pi) = -1$, so $q = 0$ at the M-line—the M-line is a void. The observed M-line is indeed a region of low protein density (the "H-zone" in relaxed muscle).

This suggests that the A-band's high $q$ corresponds to the dense, coherent myosin filament overlap region, while the I-band's low $q$ corresponds to the less dense actin-only region. The M-line's protein composition (M-band proteins, creatine kinase, etc.) may reflect a void boundary structure—a region where the condensate's coherence is minimal, populated by structural proteins that stabilize the lattice but do not themselves participate in the active contractile mechanism.

### 4.4 The 1.70$\times$ Anisotropy at Fascial Boundaries

**Prediction M4 (most immediately testable):** The edge steepness anisotropy—the ratio of $1.70\times$ in the gradient of the condensation field at bubble boundaries—should be measurable at fascial planes using ultrasound shear-wave elastography (SWE).

**Test protocol:**
- Select a superficial muscle with well-defined fascial boundaries (e.g., biceps brachii, rectus femoris).
- Acquire SWE maps of tissue stiffness in a 2D grid spanning the fascial boundary between adjacent muscles.
- Compute the spatial gradient of shear modulus across the boundary in two directions: (a) perpendicular to the fascial plane (axial direction, Yin axis), and (b) at 45° to the fascial plane (diagonal direction).
- The ratio of the two gradients—axial divided by diagonal—should be $\sqrt{4\varphi^2/(1+\varphi^2)} \approx 1.70 \pm \text{measurement uncertainty}$.

**Confounders:** SWE measures shear modulus, not Qi density directly. The correspondence between tissue stiffness and $q$ is hypothesized but not derived. However, if $q$ tracks coherence of the condensate, and coherence maps to tissue mechanical properties (via cross-linking, hydration, protein density), then the anisotropy of the gradient should be preserved in the elastography measurement even if the absolute $q$ mapping is unknown.

**Data availability:** SWE is a clinical standard for liver fibrosis assessment and is increasingly used in musculoskeletal imaging. Published fascial elastography data can be re-analyzed for the predicted anisotropy without new acquisitions.

## 6. Open Questions

1. **What sets $\theta_{\text{cond}}$ at the sarcomere scale?** The condensation threshold $\theta_{\text{cond}}$ is determined by the conversion-diffusion balance (`foundations/bubble-edge-geometry.md` §1.2). At the cosmological scale, $\theta_{\text{cond}} \approx 0.45$ is calibrated from void catalogs. At the sarcomere scale (~$n = 135$), the effective diffusion $D_{\text{eff}}$ of the condensation field in the cellular milieu is unknown. The A-band/I-band ratio ($\sim 0.6$ in human sarcomeres) can be used to infer $\theta_{\text{cond}}$ at this scale once the mapping between $q$ and protein density is established.

2. **How does contraction modify the lattice?** Muscle contraction shortens the sarcomere from ~2.5 $\mu$m to ~1.8 $\mu$m (in human skeletal muscle). In the bubble lattice picture, this is a compression of the along-string period. Does the lattice remain coherent under compression, or does it undergo a phase transition (bubble collapse, void widening)? The PDE at $n \approx 135$ with a time-varying boundary condition (simulating contraction) could answer this.

3. **Is the 1D sarcomere lattice coupled to the 3D fascial lattice?** The sarcomere is contained within the myofibril, which is within the fiber, which is within the fascicle. The bubble lattice at each scale is nested within the lattice at the next. How does the along-string periodicity at the sarcomere level (Z-disc spacing, $P_\parallel = 2$ rungs) relate to the transverse checkerboard at the fascicle level? Is the coupling between scales merely geometric (the nesting of `foundations/bubble-lattice-fabric.md` §3.2) or are there active PDE-driven interactions?

4. **Does smooth muscle show the same lattice?** Smooth muscle lacks the regular sarcomere banding of skeletal and cardiac muscle. If the bubble lattice is universal, smooth muscle should still show periodic structure—perhaps at a coarser scale (dense bodies as Z-disc analogues, caveolae as void-boundary features) or in a less regular arrangement (the checkerboard's off-lattice configurations at low $q$). The absence of regular striations may itself be informative: it would indicate that the condensation field at the smooth-muscle scale operates below the condensation threshold $q < \theta_{\text{cond}}$ or with a different $P_\parallel$. This is a natural comparative test: if both smooth and skeletal muscle obey the same condensation field, their different morphologies arise from different dynamical regimes of the same PDE, not from different organizing principles.

5. **Can cardiac muscle reveal the Fibonacci spiral?** Cardiac muscle shares skeletal muscle's sarcomere structure but differs in its branching syncytial organization. The Fibonacci spiral at octave boundaries (universal signature $4$ in `foundations/bubble-lattice-fabric.md` §4.4) may appear in the helical arrangement of cardiac myofibers—the "ventricular myocardial band" (Torrent-Guasp). The predicted 5-fold spiral arrangement in the ventricular wall is testable by diffusion-tensor MRI.
