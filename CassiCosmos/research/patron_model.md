# Cassi Open Lab Patron Model

## Status: Proposal—planning only

## 1. Summary

The **Cassi Open Lab** is a patronage model for funding continued Cassi development without investor control, closed foundational software, or pay-to-influence research.

A patron pays for the continued existence of a public project and receives communication, recognition, previews, and community access. A patron does **not** purchase equity, decision power, exclusive intellectual property, private scientific conclusions, or stronger access to a Cassi field.

The model is deliberately simpler than a startup financing structure:

- the human owner retains doctrine, public-facing words, roadmap authority, and repository control;
- foundational code, protocols, documentation, and reproducible results remain public once licensing and provenance are settled;
- patrons fund time, hardware, maintenance, experiments, documentation, and public releases;
- patrons may offer non-binding suggestions, but they cannot direct conclusions or veto work;
- null, failed, and contradictory results remain legitimate public deliverables.

This model is intended to complement the product portfolio in [`product_ideas.md`](product_ideas.md), including Cassi Cosmos Studio, the small Cassi Instruments, and the cooperative game concept in [`cassi_coop_game_design.md`](cassi_coop_game_design.md). Patronage funds the open work that makes those products possible; it does not turn patrons into customers who own the foundations.

## 2. Why patronage fits Cassi

Cassi is a long-horizon project with several kinds of work that are valuable before they become conventional products:

- GPU and field-engine maintenance;
- reproducible verification and falsification;
- open documentation and visual explainers;
- interaction and replay infrastructure;
- local-first AI and memory integration;
- public experiments whose result cannot be guaranteed in advance;
- small public instruments and game prototypes;
- hardware, build, recording, and distribution costs.

A conventional investor expects financial return, influence, or an eventual liquidity event. A patron expects the work to continue and wants to help make it possible. That relationship matches the project's actual needs and the owner's requirement to preserve final decision authority.

Patronage also lets the project stay honest. A patron funds a process and a public workstream, not a predetermined scientific conclusion.

## 3. Patron covenant

The public patron covenant should be short enough to publish on every support page:

> Patron support funds open Cassi development, maintenance, documentation, experiments, and public releases. It does not buy equity, ownership, governance, exclusivity, a roadmap veto, private research conclusions, or stronger field influence. The owner retains final authority over doctrine, public language, priorities, and releases. Results—including NULL, FAIL, REJECT, and CONTRADICTS outcomes—may be published when they are the correct result of the registered work.

### 3.1 What patron support provides

Depending on tier, patrons may receive:

- development notes and public progress summaries;
- early previews of public work;
- invitations to group demonstrations or lab briefings;
- name or organization acknowledgment, if desired;
- supporter credits in released builds or videos;
- access to patron-only discussion of already-public work;
- a voice for non-binding suggestions;
- occasional patron polls about presentation or educational priorities;
- public receipts for sponsored modules.

These are recognition and community benefits. They are not control rights.

### 3.2 What patron support does not provide

Patron support does not provide:

- shares, equity, options, SAFEs, tokens, or convertible debt;
- revenue share or profit participation;
- board, observer, or advisory control;
- roadmap, doctrine, wording, hiring, or publication vetoes;
- exclusive forks or private versions of foundational code;
- delayed publication of inconvenient results;
- private access to unpublished human, health, or sensitive personal data;
- paid influence over a shared field or game mechanic;
- scientific, therapeutic, consciousness, or cosmological validation;
- unlimited support, bespoke implementation, or guaranteed milestones.

A patron who wants bespoke engineering, integration, installation, or private deployment should use a separate service agreement with explicit scope and pricing. Patronage must not quietly become an unlimited consulting retainer.

## 4. Patron tiers

These are starting hypotheses for a public support page, not promises until the benefits and delivery cadence are tested.

### 4.1 Individual patrons

| Tier | Monthly amount | Benefit boundary |
|---|---:|---|
| **Observer** | $5 | Monthly public lab digest, patron roll acknowledgment, and access to public release notes in one convenient stream |
| **Supporter** | $15 | Observer benefits plus a monthly behind-the-scenes note, patron credit, and access to recorded public briefings |
| **Sustainer** | $50 | Supporter benefits plus quarterly group lab briefing, early public build previews, and a non-binding question submitted for a briefing |
| **Founding Patron** | $100 | Sustainer benefits plus a permanent founding acknowledgment, subject to opt-in, and one annual small-group demonstration |

The individual tiers should remain useful without creating a private support queue. One-to-one debugging, implementation, and deployment belong in paid services.

### 4.2 Organizational patrons

| Tier | Monthly amount | Public benefit |
|---|---:|---|
| **Lab Friend** | $250 | Organization acknowledgment, quarterly public briefing invitation, and a named general-maintenance support line |
| **Workstream Patron** | $500 | Lab Friend benefits plus acknowledgment on one public workstream and a quarterly progress receipt for that workstream |
| **Open Lab Sponsor** | $1,000+ | Workstream Patron benefits plus a jointly agreed public milestone or infrastructure module, with no control over its result |

A workstream sponsor chooses a **topic area**, not a conclusion. For example, a sponsor may support “interactive field tooling,” “GPU reproducibility,” or “open local AI infrastructure.” It may not require “prove that the field improves model quality.”

The minimum organizational term should be one month unless a separate written agreement is necessary for accounting or grant administration. There should be no automatic right to renew, expand, or influence the next workstream.

### 4.3 One-time patronage

One-time patronage is useful for patrons who do not want recurring payments:

- **$25–$100:** named or anonymous general support;
- **$250–$1,000:** equipment, documentation, or public-release support;
- **$2,500–$15,000:** a bounded public module or development season.

A one-time contribution funds the stated public purpose. It does not purchase a deliverable with guaranteed scientific or commercial success. If the work changes direction because a gate returns NULL or CONTRADICTS, the public receipt should say so rather than quietly substitute a different claim.

## 5. Benefit design

### 5.1 Benefits that scale well

The patron model should prefer benefits that can be delivered once and shared widely:

- monthly written lab notes;
- a quarterly recorded demonstration;
- public build and experiment receipts;
- supporter credits in releases;
- early access to public previews;
- curated seed packs or replay cards;
- public architecture diagrams;
- occasional group Q&A sessions;
- a patron changelog showing what the money made possible.

### 5.2 Benefits to avoid

Avoid benefits that create hidden labor or obligations:

- unlimited private chat;
- guaranteed response times;
- private custom builds inside a low-cost tier;
- private scientific consultations presented as findings;
- patron votes that bind the roadmap;
- paywalled bug fixes to the public foundation;
- donor-only gameplay power;
- access to sensitive user data or unpublished experiment records;
- a promise that every month produces a feature.

### 5.3 Recognition policy

A patron may choose:

- public name;
- organization name;
- pseudonym;
- anonymous support.

Recognition must not imply endorsement of every Cassi claim, nor imply scientific validation. Sponsor badges should say **Patron**, **Supporter**, or **Open Lab Sponsor**, not “verified by” or “scientific partner.”

## 6. Funding buckets and transparency

The first public funding page should show a simple monthly allocation rather than pretending to offer corporate-grade accounting before the project needs it.

A planning allocation could be:

| Bucket | Planning share | Examples |
|---|---:|---|
| Founder development time | 55% | coding, design, integration, release work |
| Hardware and infrastructure | 15% | GPU upkeep, storage, domains, build/signing, hosting |
| Verification and documentation | 15% | receipts, experiments, docs, visual explainers |
| Community and distribution | 10% | demos, recordings, accessibility, public briefings |
| Reserve | 5% | refunds, unexpected hardware, legal/accounting review |

These percentages are a budgeting baseline, not a donor promise. Publish the actual broad allocation quarterly and explain material deviations.

A quarterly patron receipt should contain:

1. opening and closing patron income for the period;
2. broad spending buckets;
3. public work completed;
4. work in progress;
5. work blocked or stopped;
6. relevant PASS, NULL, FAIL, REJECT, or CONTRADICTS outcomes;
7. next-quarter priorities, clearly marked as plans.

The receipt should never imply that a positive result was purchased by a particular patron.

## 7. Public workstreams patrons can fund

The initial menu should stay small. Suggested public workstreams are:

### 7.1 Open Field Workbench

Maintain and extend the bounded interactive field/replay layer, with every new operation receiving its own verification protocol.

**Possible public outputs:** documented commands, replay examples, measurement cards, and a small standalone field instrument.

### 7.2 Cassi Cosmos Studio

Build shared canvas, seeds, replay, recording, and export infrastructure that can support Emberloom, Paper Tide, Rungwell, and later Studio modes.

**Possible public outputs:** a signed demo build, deterministic seed pack, and an export workflow.

### 7.3 Tideweavers prototype

Prototype the bounded 2–4 player cooperative field game described in [`cassi_coop_game_design.md`](cassi_coop_game_design.md).

**Possible public outputs:** a single-player mechanic, a local two-client room, a replay receipt, and a measured mobile feasibility report.

### 7.4 GPU reproducibility

Publish focused probes, hardware notes, and failure receipts for Godot/RenderingDevice field systems.

**Possible public outputs:** minimal repro scenes, supported-hardware notes, and open fixes.

### 7.5 Open local intelligence

Package the measured local Qwen/CassiCore integration as reproducible local infrastructure without claiming field-enhanced model quality before a valid gate supports it.

**Possible public outputs:** installation receipts, model/provenance documentation, bounded memory integration, and evaluation reports.

A patron may choose a workstream, but the owner retains the right to redirect it when prerequisites, licensing, technical results, or safety concerns require a change.

## 8. The non-control sponsor agreement

Every organizational patron should receive a short written agreement containing these clauses:

1. **Nature of payment:** support, sponsorship, or patronage; not equity, debt, investment, or revenue share.
2. **No ownership:** the patron receives no ownership of Cassi code, theory, trademarks, data, or future products.
3. **Open-release expectation:** foundational work intended for the public commons will be released under the project's chosen licenses once provenance and licensing are complete.
4. **No exclusivity:** the patron receives no exclusive access, field of use, geography, customer class, or publication window.
5. **No control:** the patron has no veto or approval right over doctrine, roadmap, methods, results, public language, hiring, or release timing.
6. **No guaranteed result:** a supported experiment may return any registered verdict, including NULL, FAIL, REJECT, or CONTRADICTS.
7. **Acknowledgment:** recognition is optional and does not imply endorsement or validation.
8. **Data boundaries:** no patron receives private user data, sensitive personal information, or unrelated internal records.
9. **Termination:** either side may end recurring support under the stated payment terms; there is no obligation to continue a workstream indefinitely.
10. **Public accountability:** the project may publish a high-level receipt describing funded work without disclosing confidential patron information.

The agreement should be reviewed for the relevant jurisdiction before taking substantial organizational funds. Do not promise tax deductibility unless a qualified fiscal host or accountant confirms it.

## 9. Patron operations

### 9.1 Lightweight launch stack

Start with:

- one public patron page;
- one payment channel;
- one patron ledger;
- one quarterly receipt location;
- one mailing list or release-note stream;
- one public workstream board.

Do not build a custom patron portal, social network, token, NFT layer, or private dashboard before the basic relationship is working.

The payment platform can change later. The covenant, receipt format, and public workstream accounting should remain portable.

### 9.2 Delivery cadence

A sustainable minimum cadence is:

- **monthly:** short public lab note and patron acknowledgment update;
- **quarterly:** financial/workstream receipt and a recorded briefing;
- **per workstream:** a public preregistration or scope note before a consequential run;
- **after a run:** raw result, verdict, and limitations;
- **annually:** review tiers, costs, benefits, and whether the model is consuming too much development time.

A missed feature is not a breach if the project publishes the reason and updates the plan. Silent disappearance is the trust failure to avoid.

### 9.3 Patron communication rules

Public communications should:

- distinguish measured, implemented, proposed, and speculative content;
- use present-state language;
- link to receipts and source where possible;
- disclose when a workstream is patron-supported;
- state when a result is inconclusive or blocked;
- never use a patron's support as evidence that a theory is true.

Private patron communications should not become a parallel doctrine channel. If an idea belongs in the public research program, it should enter through the same preregistration and review process as any other idea.

## 10. Launch plan

### Phase 0: prerequisites

Before publicly calling the foundations open source or offering institutional patronage:

1. choose explicit software licenses for each active repository;
2. resolve third-party code, model, audio, image, and dependency provenance;
3. resolve the current CassiCraft `ARR` metadata and any incompatible asset terms;
4. publish a trademark and acknowledgment policy;
5. separate patron support from paid services and future product sales;
6. prepare a one-page patron covenant and privacy notice.

Source visibility without a license is not sufficient for an open-source promise.

### Phase 1: founding patron test

Invite a small group of people who already understand the project:

- ten to twenty individual patrons;
- one or two non-controlling organizational patrons;
- one public workstream;
- one month of support before adding more tiers.

The first test should measure whether patrons value continuity, transparency, and participation—not whether they can be persuaded by a grand claim.

### Phase 2: public launch

Publish:

- the covenant;
- tier table;
- current workstream menu;
- first monthly budget target;
- expected communication cadence;
- license/provenance status;
- explicit non-claims;
- cancellation and privacy terms.

A reasonable initial validation target is **$500–$1,000 in recurring monthly support** with no patron requiring an unsustainable private service burden. Treat that as a test threshold, not a forecast.

### Phase 3: institutional expansion

Only after the individual model is stable:

- add $250–$1,000 organizational tiers;
- accept bounded module sponsorships;
- publish quarterly receipts;
- consider a fiscal host for grants or tax administration;
- keep every sponsor agreement non-controlling and non-exclusive.

## 11. Success metrics

Track the model with a small public dashboard or quarterly table:

- recurring monthly patron income;
- one-time patron income;
- number of active patrons;
- retention and cancellation rate;
- hours spent on patron benefits and support;
- number of public releases or receipts;
- percentage of funds spent on development versus administration;
- number of workstreams that returned honest negative results;
- number of patrons who converted to a separate paid service without confusing the two relationships.

The patron model is working if it increases uninterrupted open development without creating hidden support labor or pressure to overclaim.

## 12. Red lines

The following are incompatible with this model:

- equity, SAFEs, convertible notes, tokens, or profit-sharing presented as patronage;
- sponsor vetoes, board seats, observer rights, or roadmap approval;
- a patron-only solver, stronger field, or exclusive foundational branch;
- withholding a NULL or contradictory result because it displeases a sponsor;
- selling therapeutic, consciousness, compatibility, or human-energy interpretations;
- accepting sensitive user data as a patron benefit;
- donor-funded claims presented as independent validation;
- lifetime unlimited support or unlimited hosting;
- pay-to-win mechanics in the cooperative game;
- public claims that patron payments are tax-deductible without qualified confirmation.

## 13. Suggested public copy

> **Support the Cassi Open Lab**
>
> Cassi is an open research and software project exploring field-based simulation, local intelligence infrastructure, interactive worlds, and reproducible experiments. Patrons help fund the time, hardware, documentation, and public releases that keep the work moving.
>
> Your support does not buy ownership, governance, exclusive access, or a preferred scientific result. The foundations remain public as licensing and provenance are completed. We publish what works, what fails, and what remains unresolved.
>
> Patron benefits include development notes, public receipts, previews, demonstrations, and optional acknowledgment. If you want custom engineering, deployment, or a commissioned visualization, that is a separate service.
